RMD rules and start age

What is an RMD?

An RMD is a required minimum distribution. It is the smallest amount the tax rules require to be taken out of certain retirement accounts each year once the owner reaches the start age. The start age is the age when RMDs begin.

The calculator uses the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table; the Joint Life table, used when the only beneficiary is a spouse more than 10 years younger, is not included in this version.

Which accounts have RMDs?

Traditional IRAs, SEP IRAs, SIMPLE IRAs and employer plans such as a 401(k) have RMDs. Employer plans can have extra rules of their own, so the plan administrator can confirm how a plan handles it.

Roth IRAs are different. The IRS says: "If you are the original owner of a Roth IRA, you don't have to take distributions regardless of your age."

The IRS FAQ page covers Roth accounts in employer plans too: "The RMD rules do not apply to Roth IRAs or Designated Roth accounts while the owner is alive." The lifetime exemption for Roth accounts in employer plans applies to taxable years beginning after December 31, 2023.

Source: IRS Publication 590-B, 2025 edition; IRS, Retirement plan and IRA required minimum distributions FAQs (question 2 updated Dec. 10, 2024; page last reviewed or updated 29 Jan 2026); and Internal Revenue Bulletin 2024-33 (12 Aug 2024), read 14 Nov 2026.

When do RMDs start?

IRS Publication 590-B gives the start age as "Age 73 for tax years 2023 and later."

The start age also depends on the year of birth. This part is from the final regulations and a proposed rule, not stated on the Publication 590-B page.

  • Born from 1951 to 1958: age 73.
  • Born in 1960 or later: age 75.
  • Born in 1959: the final regulations leave 1959 open. A proposed rule says age 73. We found no final rule for 1959 when we read the IRS pages on 14 Nov 2026.

Source: IRS Publication 590-B, 2025 edition; IRS, Internal Revenue Bulletin 2024-33 (12 Aug 2024), final regulations T.D. 10001 and the related proposed rule, read 14 Nov 2026. The birth-year ages are from the final regulations and a proposed rule, not stated on the Publication 590-B page.

How is an RMD worked out with the Uniform Lifetime Table?

The IRS divides the account balance at the close of business on 31 December of the year before by a number called the distribution period, or divisor. The divisor comes from an IRS life expectancy table. The most common one is the Uniform Lifetime Table.

Worked example: an account held $100,000 on 31 December. The owner is 75, and the table gives a distribution period of 24.6. $100,000 divided by 24.6 is $4,065.04. The IRS rounds to whole dollars in its examples, so it shows $4,065.

Source: IRS Publication 590-B, 2025 edition, and its example for 2026 distributions, read 14 Nov 2026.

Can the first RMD be delayed?

The first RMD can be delayed. The IRS says any amount taken in the year a person reaches the start age "will be credited toward the amount that must be distributed by April 1" of the next year. Later RMDs are due by 31 December.

That means a delayed first payment brings two payments into the same next year: the first RMD and that year's own RMD. The calculator's table lists each year's RMD on the 31 December basis and does not model two payments.

Source: IRS Publication 590-B, 2025 edition, and IRS RMD FAQs (page last reviewed or updated 29 Jan 2026), read 14 Nov 2026.

What happens if an RMD is missed?

The IRS describes a 25% excise tax (an extra tax) on the amount not taken. If the shortfall is corrected in time, the IRS describes a reduced additional tax rate of 10%.

The IRS FAQ puts it this way: the 10% rate applies "if the RMD is timely corrected within two years." Publication 590-B gives the exact end of that window: the earliest of the date a notice of deficiency is mailed, the date the tax is assessed, or "the last day of the second taxable year that begins after the end of the taxable year in which the additional tax is imposed."

If the shortfall was due to reasonable error, the IRS may waive the tax. Publication 590-B describes how to ask: complete Form 5329 and attach a statement of explanation.

Source: IRS Publication 590-B, 2025 edition; IRS, Retirement plan and IRA required minimum distributions FAQs (question 8 updated Dec. 10, 2024; page last reviewed or updated 29 Jan 2026), read 14 Nov 2026.

Roth conversions and RMDs

What is a Roth conversion?

A Roth conversion moves money from a traditional IRA into a Roth IRA. Most money in a traditional IRA has not been taxed yet. The Roth IRA has no RMDs for its original owner, as shown above.

Is a conversion taxed?

Apart from any part that was already taxed, the converted amount is normally included in income for the year of the conversion. IRS Publication 590-A says these amounts are "normally included in income on your return for the year that you converted them."

Source: IRS Publication 590-A, 2025 edition, read 14 Nov 2026.

Can an RMD be converted?

No. IRS Publication 590-A says: "You can't convert amounts that must be distributed from your traditional IRA for a particular year." Publication 590-B says: "Amounts that must be distributed (required minimum distributions) during a particular year aren't eligible for rollover treatment."

Source: IRS Publications 590-A and 590-B, 2025 editions, read 14 Nov 2026.

Why would a smaller balance mean a smaller RMD?

For the same age the divisor is the same, so a smaller balance gives a smaller result.

Example only, with made-up numbers. This is not a promise or a forecast. Take a divisor of 24.6.

  • A $100,000 balance gives $100,000 divided by 24.6, which is $4,065 to the nearest dollar.
  • An $80,000 balance gives $80,000 divided by 24.6, which is $3,252 to the nearest dollar.

This leaves out the tax paid on the conversion. Moving money out of the traditional IRA before RMD age can reduce the balance the formula works from. Whether that happens depends on the account, the markets and the rules in force in each year.

For education only, not financial advice.

What else can a conversion change?
  • The tax bracket. A conversion adds to income for that year. A larger income can move part of it into a higher bracket (a bracket is an income range; each extra dollar in it is taxed at that bracket's rate).
  • Medicare premiums. A higher income can raise them. The extra charge is called IRMAA, short for income-related monthly adjustment amount. It is generally based on a tax return from two years earlier: the Social Security Administration says the information for 2026 premiums is generally from a tax return filed in 2025 for tax year 2024. The standard Part B premium for 2026 is $202.90 a month, or higher depending on income. The Social Security Administration publishes the income thresholds each year.

Source: Social Security Administration, Medicare premiums page, and Medicare.gov, Medicare costs page, read 14 Nov 2026. Threshold and add-on amounts are not given here.

What are the two five-year rules?

People often mix these up. They are separate rules.

  1. The conversion rule. If converted amounts are taken out "within the 5-year period starting with the first day of your tax year in which you convert," the 10% additional tax on early distributions may apply. There are exceptions, such as having reached age 59½; Publication 590-B lists them. Each conversion has its own five-year period.
  2. The Roth IRA rule for earnings. A Roth distribution is qualified only if it is made after "the 5-year period beginning with the first tax year for which a contribution was made" and also meets another condition, such as being made on or after the date you reach age 59½.

Source: IRS Publication 590-B, 2025 edition, read 14 Nov 2026.

Can a conversion cost more than it saves?

The tax paid on a conversion can be larger than any later tax saved. Higher income in the conversion year can also carry costs, such as the bracket and Medicare effects above. This page does not say whether a conversion suits anyone.
