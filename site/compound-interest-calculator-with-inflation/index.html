Future dollars and today's dollars

The balance on a statement is in future dollars (also called nominal dollars): the number of dollars in the account on that date. Today's dollars turn that number into what it would buy at today's prices.

The chart shows both, side by side. The gap between the two lines is what inflation would take from the balance's buying power. The longer the time, the wider the gap.

"Money put in" is the plain total of your deposits, as paid. It is not adjusted for inflation.

How inflation is applied

Each year's balance is divided by how much prices would have grown by then:

today's dollars = balance ÷ (1 + inflation)years

Inflation is a yearly rate, compounded once a year, just like a price rising by the same percentage every year.

Worked example. $10,000 at an example rate of 5% compounded monthly, with nothing added, would grow to $16,470.09 in 10 years. With an example inflation rate of 3%:

  1. Price growth over 10 years. 1.0310 = 1.343916. Prices would be about 1.34 times what they are today.
  2. Divide. 16,470.09 ÷ 1.343916 = $12,255.30.

So $16,470.09 in 10 years would buy about what $12,255.30 buys today.

The real rate after inflation

The real rate (sometimes called the real rate of return) is how fast buying power grows once inflation is taken out. The exact formula is:

real rate = (1 + rate) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1

Worked example. An account growing at an example rate of 5% a year, with an example inflation rate of 3%:

1.05 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = 0.0194, which is an example real rate of 1.94%.

A common shortcut is to subtract the example rates: 5% − 3% = 2%. That is close, but it is only an approximation. The gap gets bigger as the rates get bigger.

Why the calculator shows 2.05% for the same example rates of 5% and 3%. It uses the yearly growth once compounding is counted, called the APY. An example rate of 5% compounded monthly has an APY of 5.12%, so the example real rate is 1.0511619 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = 2.05%. That keeps the real rate in step with the today's-dollars figures.

When the two rates are equal. $10,000 at an example rate of 5% compounded annually, with nothing added and an example inflation rate of 5%, shows $10,000.00 in today's dollars every year. The example real rate is 0.00%. The balance rises, but only as fast as prices.

At 0% inflation, today's dollars equal the balance, and the real rate equals the APY (5.12% for an example rate of 5% compounded monthly).

So the statement would show $31,998.32, and that balance would buy about what $23,809.76 buys today.

Choosing an inflation rate

Future inflation is unknown. The 3% starting value is only an example, not a forecast.

Because no one knows the future rate, it helps to try more than one. For example, run the same savings at a low inflation rate and a higher one, and look at how far apart the today's-dollars results are. That range says more than any single answer.

Inflation also differs by what you buy. Rent, food and fuel prices can each change at their own pace.

How this calculator works

The balance. The first six inputs go through the same calculation as our compound interest calculator, unchanged. So the balance, the money put in and the interest match it to the cent for the same inputs. For quarterly or annual compounding with monthly contributions, that calculation follows a common bank convention, described in how the compound interest calculator works.

Inflation. It is a yearly rate, compounded once a year. Each year-end balance is divided by (1 + inflation)years. Only year-end figures are shown, so no within-year inflation is assumed.

The real rate. (1 + APY) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1, where the APY is the yearly growth of your interest rate at the compounding you chose.

Rounding. Money is shown to the cent. The real rate is rounded to 2 decimal places, with halves rounded up.

What it leaves out. Your monthly contribution stays the same number of dollars every year. It does not rise with inflation. Taxes and fees are not included. If you know the APY a savings account advertises, the savings calculator takes it directly.

Everything runs in your browser, and nothing you type is sent to us.
