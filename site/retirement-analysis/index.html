    How the simulation works

    • 500 runs. Each run builds a possible future one year at a time by drawing past years at random from the history window below; every asset class uses the same drawn year.
    • All figures are in today's dollars: each drawn year's return is reduced by that year's inflation.
    • The mix is rebalanced to its fixed split every year. No fees or fund costs are taken off.
    • Contributions are added at the end of each year until the year before retirement.
    • Recurring income counts from its own start age. If it starts before retirement, it is saved until retirement.
    • Social Security, pension and annuity amounts stay the same in today's dollars every year, as if they rose with inflation. Many pensions and annuities do not, so their real value would fall over time.
    • Total lifetime income adds up Social Security, pensions and annuities over the retirement years. Other recurring income and one-time income count in total income, not in lifetime income.
    • In retirement, withdrawals come out at the start of the year in this order: any required minimum distribution from tax-deferred savings, then taxable, then more tax-deferred, then tax-exempt. This order is a fixed rule of the simulation, not a suggestion about the order to use.
    • Tax is one rate that you type, applied to withdrawals from tax-deferred savings. It is an assumption, not tax advice. Tax on a required minimum distribution that is not needed for spending and is put back into savings is shown on its own and is not counted in expenses.
    • Required minimum distributions use the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table and begin at the start age for your birth year, even while still working. The still-working exception for workplace plans is not applied, so the page may show distributions earlier than a workplace plan would require. The option to take the first one by 1 April of the next year is not modelled.
    • The money lasted means savings and income covered every year's expenses and tax to the plan-to age.
    • The three outcomes are chosen by how each run's plan ended, ranked from worst to best. They rank plan results, not markets.
    • The random draws use a fixed seed, so the same inputs always give the same 500 runs. That makes results repeatable; it does not make them more accurate.
    • With 500 runs, a count near the middle can move by about 22 runs either way from one set of runs to another.

    What is not modelled

    • A partner or spouse, and the joint life table for required minimum distributions
    • Future, inflated dollars
    • Health-care costs rising faster than other prices
    • Federal brackets, state and local taxes, and filing status
    • An estimate of Social Security: you type the amount
    • Buying or pricing an annuity
    • Pensions and annuities that do not keep up with inflation
    • Tax on Social Security, pension, annuity and other income: none is taken off in this illustration
    • Fees, fund costs and taxes on growth in taxable accounts
    • Linked accounts and saved plans
    • The still-working exception for workplace plans

    This illustration sees only what is typed here. Other assets, income and investments are part of the full picture and are worth weighing alongside it.

      Which outcome to show

      These are what-if settings, not forecasts. Outcomes worse than any of them are possible. Each shows one simulated run, drawn from the history listed under the assumptions. The 500 runs are ranked by how the plan ended. Runs that ran short rank below runs that lasted. Among runs that ran short, the one whose first shortfall came earliest ranks worst, then the one with the larger total shortfall. Among runs that lasted, less money left at the end ranks lower. This ranks plan results, not markets: a run can have ordinary markets and still end badly if weak years come early. When runs end the same, they tie, so the counts below say at least and at most.
